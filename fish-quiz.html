🎓 Techniques

What It Is

Spin fishing uses a spinning or baitcasting reel to cast weighted lures or bait. It's versatile — you can fish rivers, lakes, ocean surf, and estuaries. Most anglers in Clatsop County start here.

Spinning vs. Baitcasting Reels

Spinning reels (hang under the rod): Easier to use, great for lighter lines and finesse techniques. The bail flips to release line. The spool is fixed and doesn't rotate — the line peels off the end. Best choice for beginners and most freshwater fishing.

Baitcasting reels (sit on top of rod): More precise for heavy applications (salmon, bass) and can cast heavier lures more accurately. The spool rotates during the cast — thumb control prevents "backlash" (bird's nest). Steeper learning curve but preferred by experienced anglers for power fishing.

💡 Pro Setup
Use braid as your main line (say 30lb) with a fluorocarbon leader (12-15lb, 3-6ft) tied on with a Double Uni knot. Best of both worlds.
Leaders

A leader is a length of usually-fluorocarbon line between your main line and your hook/lure. Leaders serve multiple purposes: abrasion resistance near rocks, invisibility in clear water, and a weak link that breaks before your main line if snagged. For salmon, use 20-30lb fluoro leaders 2-4ft long. For trout, use 6-10lb fluoro 2-3ft long.

Terminal Tackle
  • Swivels: Barrel swivels prevent line twist from spinning lures. Snap swivels allow quick lure changes. Always use a swivel between braid and fluoro leader when using rotating lures.
  • Split rings: Small wire rings that attach hooks to lure bodies. Use split ring pliers — never try to force open with fingernails.
  • Snaps: Quick-change connectors between line and lure. Convenient but add a weak point — not ideal for heavy fish.
Lure Types & How to Fish Them
  • Spinners (Blue Fox Vibrax, Mepps): Cast across current, retrieve at steady speed. Blade rotates creating flash and vibration. Excellent for salmon, steelhead, trout. Size up in high/murky water.
  • Spoons (Kastmaster, Crippled Herring): Wobbling metal lures that mimic injured baitfish. Cast and retrieve or jig vertically. Great for salmon, lingcod, tuna.
  • Crankbaits (Rapala, Shad Rap): Hard-body lures that dive to a set depth. Troll or cast and retrieve. Match depth to fish location. Good for bass, walleye, salmon trolling.
  • Soft plastics (Senko, tubes, grubs): Flexible plastic bodies on jig heads. Extremely versatile — fish near bottom for bass/walleye, mid-water for rockfish. Scented versions add attractant.
  • Jigs: Weighted hook with bucktail, marabou, or soft plastic body. Bounce along bottom, jig vertically, or swim through water column. One of the most versatile lure types.
Retrieve Techniques
  • Steady retrieve: Consistent, medium-speed reel. Basic and effective for spinners and crankbaits.
  • Stop-and-go: Reel, pause, reel, pause. Triggering for bass and trout — they often strike on the drop.
  • Jigging: Rod tip sweeps up, then drops back as you reel in slack. Creates up-down lure action. Essential for rockfish, lingcod, bottom fish.
  • Deadstick: Do absolutely nothing. Let the lure sit. Deadly for pressured fish in cold water. Trout anglers use this in winter.
Still Fishing vs. Drift Fishing

Still fishing: Casting bait to a spot and waiting. Used in lakes (trout, catfish, bass) and slow river areas (sturgeon). Gear stays in one place.

Drift fishing: Letting bait tumble naturally downstream with the current. The premier technique for steelhead and salmon in rivers. Bait should drift at the same speed as the current, ticking the bottom.

Float/Bobber Fishing

Fixed float: Float attached at a fixed point. Simple, good for still water and shallow depths (under 4 feet). Classic bobber setup.

Slip float: Float slides freely on the line; a stop bead and knot (bobber stop) halts it at your chosen depth. Allows you to cast precisely even when fishing 10+ feet deep. Essential for steelhead and salmon with jigs/eggs.

Strike detection: Watch the float intently. Quick submersion = fish. Slow movement sideways = fish moving. Bobbing/twitching can be bites — set the hook immediately.

Depth setting: Set your float so bait drifts 6-12 inches off the bottom. Too deep = constant snags. Too shallow = fish won't see it. Adjust until bait occasionally ticks bottom.

Bottom Fishing Rigs
  • Egg sinker (sliding) rig: Sinker slides freely on line — fish can pick up bait without feeling resistance. Best for lake fishing (trout, catfish, bass) where fish taste the bait before committing.
  • Carolina rig: Sinker above swivel with 12-18 inch fluorocarbon leader to hook/soft plastic. Fish the leader length off the bottom. Bass fishing staple.
  • Dropper loop (spreader) rig: Multiple hooks tied above the sinker at intervals. Great for bottomfishing multiple species at once (rockfish, perch, cod).
  • 3-way swivel rig: Main line to swivel, dropper to sinker, leader to hook. Used for sturgeon and halibut — allows bait to float up while sinker anchors.

Curing Salmon Eggs for Steelhead

Cured eggs outfish raw eggs significantly for steelhead and salmon. Basic cure: collect fresh skein (egg sack) from a harvested salmon. Cut into 1-inch clusters. Lay on newspaper or paper towels. Sprinkle borax, fine salt, and sugar over eggs. Optional: add Pautzke Fire Cure powder for bright orange color and scent. Let sit 24 hours (or microwave 30 seconds for quick cure). Eggs should be firm, slightly tacky, with good color. Store in sealed container in refrigerator (2 weeks) or freezer (6 months).

💡 Size Rule
Hook sizes get SMALLER as numbers get BIGGER for small hooks (a #10 is smaller than a #6). But for large hooks, sizes are preceded by "0" — a 4/0 is larger than a 1/0, and much larger than a #4.
What Makes Fly Fishing Different

Unlike spin fishing where the lure has weight, fly fishing uses the weight of the fly line itself to carry the nearly weightless fly to the fish. The cast is fundamentally different — you're casting the line, not the lure. It requires learning a new casting motion but opens up fishing opportunities that spin gear cannot match.

Fly Line Types
  • Weight Forward (WF): Heavy head at the front tapers to thin running line. Most common — good for most fishing. Easier to cast long distances.
  • Double Taper (DT): Thicker in the middle, tapers both ends. Delicate presentation for spooky fish. Can flip line end-for-end to double line life.
  • Shooting head: Short, heavy head designed for maximum distance casting. Used for steelhead swinging on big rivers.
  • Sinking lines: Sink-tip or full-sink lines get flies deeper. Critical for steelhead in winter.
Leaders and Tippet

Tapered leader: Pre-made monofilament section that narrows from thick (connected to fly line) to thin (at the fly). Typically 7.5 or 9 feet. The taper transfers energy smoothly to turn the fly over.

Tippet: Additional line added to the thin end of the leader to extend its life. Sizes: 4X (about 6lb) for most trout fishing, 3X (8lb) for larger trout and cutthroat, 1X or 0X (up to 15lb) for salmon/steelhead. Lower X number = heavier tippet.

Fly Types for Clatsop County
  • Dry flies: Float on the surface. Cutthroat hit dries in summer in small streams. Elk Hair Caddis, Royal Wulff, Parachute Adams.
  • Nymphs: Subsurface flies imitating invertebrate larvae. Fished under an indicator (strike indicator = fly fishing bobber). Hare's Ear, Pheasant Tail, Bead Head Stonefly.
  • Streamers: Large flies that mimic baitfish. Strip through the water on the retrieve. Woolly Bugger (the universal fly), Clouser Minnow, Sculpzilla.
  • Egg patterns: Round, colorful flies imitating salmon eggs. Essential for steelhead and cutthroat during salmon runs. Glo-bug, Nuclear Egg, Iliamna Pinkie.
  • Intruders and Spey flies: Large, flowing two-hook flies designed for swinging for steelhead. Tied with marabou, ostrich, and flowing materials. Often 4-6 inches long.
Essential Casts
  • Overhead cast: The basic cast. Back cast loads the rod; forward cast delivers the fly. Key: stop the rod at 10 o'clock on the back cast, let line fully extend, then drive forward to 2 o'clock.
  • Roll cast: No back cast — used when trees or banks prevent a full back cast. Roll the line in a D-loop beside you and shoot it forward. Essential for tight Clatsop County streams.
  • Mending: After the fly lands, flip (mend) the line upstream to prevent current from dragging the fly unnaturally. Critical for dead-drift nymphing. Upstream mend = slower drift.
Key Techniques

Dead drift nymphing: Nymph under indicator, drifting at exact current speed. Any drag = unnatural = no bite. Mend constantly to achieve perfect drift. Most effective method for trout in Clatsop County.

Swinging a wet fly: Cast across current at 45°, let fly swing in an arc below you. Classic steelhead method. Takes patience — the grab comes at the end of the swing, often a crushing take. Fish one step downstream, repeat.

Dry fly presentation: Cast above rising fish, let fly drift naturally over them. Drag = death. Use upstream presentations and long leaders (12-15ft) for spooky fish in clear water.

Best Clatsop County Fly Fishing
  • Necanicum River: Sea-run cutthroat in fall (Sept-Oct). Small stream, roll cast essential. Elk Hair Caddis or Woolly Bugger.
  • Winter steelhead rivers: Necanicum, Youngs, Klaskanine. Dec-March. Egg patterns and Intruders fished on sink-tip lines.
  • Coffenbury Lake: Bluegill and bass on poppers (surface flies) in calm summer evenings. Easy and fun intro to fly fishing.
Wading Safety
  • Wading staff: Third leg of stability. Collapsible wading staff = essential on slippery Oregon rivers. Never wade without one in fast water.
  • Felt vs. rubber soles: Felt grips smooth algae-covered rocks better. Rubber soles are fine in sandy/gravel rivers. Many waters now ban felt (invasive species transport).
  • Wading belt: On chest waders, always wear a belt around your waist. If you fall in, the belt traps air in waders keeping you buoyant. Without it, waders fill with water and can pull you under.
  • Go slow: One foot at a time. Test each step before committing weight. Face upstream to use current against you. Never turn your back to fast current.
When to Troll vs. Cast

Trolling (dragging lures or bait behind a moving boat) covers water quickly, keeping lures at a consistent depth and speed. It's the premier method for ocean salmon, lake trout, and large lake species. Casting is better in small areas with visible fish, or when fish are concentrated in one spot.

Salmon Trolling Setup

Flasher + Hoochie: A rotating flasher (11-12 inch chrome or brass plate) attached above a hoochie (squid-like rubber lure) on 18-24 inch leader. The flasher creates flash and wobble; the hoochie dances behind it. This is the standard ocean salmon rig. Fish Blue/Green flashers for Chinook, Pink/Chrome for Coho.

Whole/Cut herring rigs: A whole herring rigged on a bait rig (two hooks through the herring) trolled behind a flasher. Or "plug-cut" herring: cut the head at an angle so the herring rolls and spins as it's trolled. Rig the cut end on a double-hook setup. Very effective for Chinook.

Downriggers

A downrigger uses a heavy cannonball weight (10-15 lbs) lowered on a cable to carry your line to precise depths. Your fishing line is clipped to the cannonball via a release clip. When a fish strikes, the line pops free and you fight the fish without the weight. Essential for getting salmon down to 40-100+ feet in the ocean.

How to set depth: Lower cannonball to desired depth (displayed on downrigger counter). Attach your rod line to the downrigger clip. Let out 6-15 feet of line between clip and lure (this is the "leader" behind the cannonball). Put rod in holder and watch the tip for taps indicating a fish.

Planer Boards

Planer boards are plastic devices that run off to the side of the boat on a cord, pulling your fishing line out horizontally from the boat path. This lets you troll multiple lines that don't interfere with each other, spreading coverage across a wide area. Great for covering schools of Coho near the surface.

Trolling Speed

For salmon: 1.5-3.5 mph (measured by GPS). Coho typically prefer 2.5-3.5 mph. Chinook slower, 1.5-2.5 mph. Adjust speed and watch your flasher — it should have an aggressive wobble but not spin wildly. Use a kicker motor for precise speed control. Prop wash and current affect actual bait speed.

Dodgers vs. Flashers
  • Flasher: Rotates 360° — creates intense flash and vibration. More attractor action. Better in deep, dark water or for aggressive fish. Use for Chinook and ocean Coho.
  • Dodger: Side-to-side wobble, does NOT rotate. Imparts action to the fly/lure behind it. Softer, more subtle action. Better for trout trolling in lakes and Coho in rivers.
Columbia River Bar Safety
⚠️ Critical Safety
The Columbia River Bar is one of the most dangerous river mouths in the world — "The Graveyard of the Pacific." Hundreds of ships have sunk here. Never cross the bar without checking the NOAA marine forecast (winds and wave height), having a VHF radio, wearing a life jacket, and knowing your boat's limitations. When in doubt, don't go out. If conditions are above 4-6 foot seas, even experienced anglers stay in.
Specialized Surf Gear
  • Surf rods: 10-14 feet long (Penn Prevail II Surf, Ugly Stik Bigwater). Length allows casting over breaking waves and keeping line above them. Medium-heavy power with a fast tip.
  • Surf reels: Larger spinning reels (5000-8000 size) with high line capacity and strong drag. Penn Battle III, Shimano Baitrunner, Daiwa BG. Load with 20-30lb mono or braid.
  • Pyramid sinkers: Angular pyramid shape digs into sand, anchoring your rig in strong surf. Use 2-4 oz depending on wave and wind conditions.
  • Sand spikes: PVC tubes pounded into the sand to hold your rod while you wait. Essential for hands-free surf fishing.
Surf Rigs

High-Low rig: Two hooks tied at intervals above the sinker (one closer to sinker, one higher). Lets you fish two different depths simultaneously. Best for perch, flounder, smelt.

Fish finder rig: Sliding sinker on main line above a swivel, with 18-24 inch leader to hook. Fish can pick up bait and run without feeling the sinker weight. Good for larger species like striped bass.

Reading the Beach
  • Troughs: Depressions between the beach and outer sandbars. Fish feed in these channels. Look for darker water (indicates depth) running parallel to the beach.
  • Rip currents: Water flowing seaward through gaps in sandbars. Identify by choppy, darker water cutting perpendicular through the wave break. Dangerous for swimmers but an excellent fishing spot — baitfish and gamefish concentrate here.
  • Structure: Rock outcroppings, jetty ends, and creek mouths on the beach all concentrate fish. The Peter Iredale shipwreck at Fort Stevens is a classic example — the submerged structure holds perch.
Target Species from Clatsop Beaches
  • Redtail Surfperch: Primary target. Sand shrimp on bottom, 1-3 waves out. February-May peak.
  • Striped Bass: Occasional near Columbia River mouth. Large swimbaits and poppers in summer.
  • Starry Flounder: Sandy bottom in calmer surf, nightcrawlers.
Best Beaches
  • Seaside Beach: Gentle approach, good access, consistent surfperch fishing at the river mouth area.
  • Cannon Beach: Beautiful setting, surfperch in the troughs, rocks near Haystack provide structure.
  • Del Rey Beach: Less crowded, good surfperch fishing. Park at Del Rey Beach Road end.
  • Fort Stevens State Park: Drive-on beach area near Peter Iredale wreck. Excellent access, multiple species.
Tides and Timing

For surfperch: fish the incoming tide, especially 2 hours before to 2 hours after high tide. As the tide pushes in, water floods the beach troughs and surfperch follow the baitfish and sand crabs into the shallows. The first big incoming tide after a week of outgoing tides can be spectacular. Check NOAA tide charts (tidesandcurrents.noaa.gov) for Astoria/Columbia River entrance.

💡 Free Fishing
No fishing license is required to catch surfperch from an Oregon ocean beach (tide lands). This makes surf fishing for redtail surfperch one of the most accessible forms of fishing in the state.
Charter Boats

Charter boats operate from Warrenton (Pacific Way Marina) and Astoria (East Mooring Basin). Full-day trips run 5-8 hours. Half-day trips 4-5 hours. Charters supply rods, reels, bait, and tackle — just bring your license and food/drinks. Tip the deckhand well — they work extremely hard. Book in advance during peak season (June-September).

Key charter operators: Search "Astoria charter fishing" for current operations. Prices vary $150-$250+ per person for full-day salmon or halibut trips.

Bottomfishing: Rockfish, Lingcod, Cabezon

Bottomfishing targets species that live near the seafloor. Standard setup: a dropper loop rig with 2-3 hooks at intervals above a heavy sinker (6-16 oz). Bait with herring strips, squid, or shrimp. Drop to the bottom, reel up 3-5 feet, then jig slowly. Rockfish hit immediately when the bait reaches their depth. On charter boats, the crew will tell you the target depth.

Lingcod, cabezon, and large rockfish respond well to large rubber swimbaits (3-5 inch Berkley Powerbait Swimmers) on leadhead jigs. Drop to bottom, rip up 5 feet, let fall back. Strikes typically come on the fall.

Halibut: Drift Fishing

Pacific Halibut season is typically May-September (check ODFW annually — quota closures occur). Target depth: 40-80 fathoms (240-480 ft). Standard technique: anchor or drift over sandy bottom. Use a spreader bar rig: 80-100lb fluorocarbon leader, 8/0-10/0 circle hook, whole herring or large smelt as bait. The boat drifts, dragging bait along the bottom. When a halibut grabs the bait, don't yank — apply steady pressure and let the circle hook set itself. Fight is a deadlift from depth — halibut don't jump but large fish are like pulling a door up from 300 feet.

Offshore Salmon Trolling

Ocean Chinook and Coho are targeted 1-20 miles offshore from May-October (check ODFW for current seasons and area openings). Typical setup: downriggers set to 20-60 feet, with flasher + hoochie or whole/plug-cut herring. Speed 2-3.5 mph. Work temperature breaks (where cold and warm water meet) and look for birds diving on baitfish.

Albacore Tuna (Offshore)

Albacore fishing happens 40-80 miles offshore when warm Pacific water (59°F+) pushes inshore — typically August-October. Full-day charter trips are 12+ hours. Technique: troll at 6-9 knots with cedar plugs, feathers, and spreader bars. When a school is found, boats stop and anglers cast metal jigs or swimbaits to the tuna feeding at the surface. Action can be furious — multiple hook-ups simultaneously. Ocean boat license required in addition to regular angling license.

Ocean Safety Checklist
  • Check NOAA Marine Forecast (weather.gov/pqr) before every trip — minimum 2 days out
  • Life jacket for every person aboard — wear it in rough conditions
  • VHF Marine Radio (Channel 16 monitored for emergencies) — a cell phone is not adequate offshore
  • Float plan: tell someone where you're going and when you'll return
  • Know your boat's limitations — if it's rated for 4-foot waves, don't push it
  • Bar crossing: check USCG bar conditions (Marine Safety Radio Channel 16 or app)
Licensing: Oregon Ocean Boat License

Any vessel used for ocean sport fishing needs an Oregon Ocean Boat License ($26/year). Anglers also need a valid Oregon angling license plus a Combined Angling Tag for salmon/steelhead/sturgeon. Licenses available at ODFW offices, most sporting goods stores, and online at myodfw.com.

Dungeness Crab

Oregon's most prized crustacean. Full-season crabbing is one of Clatsop County's best outdoor traditions. Season is typically December 1 through August 14 (ODFW sets dates annually based on meat fill and safety — verify before every trip).

Gear: Ring Nets vs. Pots
  • Ring nets: Two rings connected by mesh, baited and dropped to the bottom. Pull up quickly every 20-30 minutes. Legal from shore and piers without a boat. Easy to use.
  • Crab pots: Rigid wire cages with entrance funnels. Left on bottom for hours. More effective per pull but require a boat and license. Pots must have escape rings and ID tags.
Bait for Crab
  • Chicken legs/backs: Cheap, readily available, effective. Smell draws crabs from a distance.
  • Fish carcasses: After filleting a fish, freeze the carcass and use for crab bait. Free and very effective.
  • Commercial crab bait (Dungeness crab attractant): Available at local shops. Concentrated scent.
Regulations and Measuring

Minimum size: 5¾ inches across the widest part of the carapace (shell). Measure with calipers. Do not estimate — undersized crab must go back immediately.

Males only: Only male (buck) Dungeness can be retained. Females have a wider, rounded abdomen flap; males have a narrow, pointed flap. Female crabs, small males, and soft-shelled crabs must be released immediately and gently.

Bag limit: 12 Dungeness crab per day. Shellfish license required (included in combo license or $10 add-on for non-license holders).

Best Crabbing Spots in Clatsop County
  • South Jetty (Fort Stevens): Ring nets from the jetty rocks. Walk from the parking area. Highly productive.
  • Youngs Bay: From boat — large bay with excellent crab habitat. Launch from Astoria waterfront.
  • Columbia River estuary channels: Deeper channels hold large crab. Boat access required.
Razor Clams

Oregon razor clams are a prized delicacy found in sandy beach surf zones. Licensing: regular Oregon angling license + shellfish endorsement required. Season: typically October through May, with specific beach openings set by ODFW after biotoxin (paralytic shellfish poisoning) testing. Always verify current beach openings at myodfw.com — biotoxin closures can occur without warning and are a health hazard.

How to Dig Razor Clams

Clam gun (tube): Insert tube over the clam's breathing hole (a dimple in the sand), push down, cover the air hole with thumb, pull up — captures a core of sand with the clam. Easier for beginners.

Shovel: More traditional. Dig quickly beside the dimple (not on it), tilt sand toward you, reach down and grab the clam before it retreats. Fast hands required — razors can retreat 6 inches in seconds.

Finding clams: Look for dimples (round depressions) or keyholes (oval depressions) in wet sand near the water's edge at low tide. Tap the sand with your foot — clams retreat, leaving a more visible dimple. Best tides: minus tides (below zero) expose the most beach.

Best beaches: Long Beach, WA (just north) and Cannon Beach/Seaside areas when open. Fort Stevens beach at low tide.

Every angler needs 5 reliable knots. Practice them at home until you can tie them in the dark, in the rain, with cold hands. A poorly tied knot is the #1 cause of lost fish.

1. Improved Clinch Knot — Attaching lure or hook to monofilament/fluorocarbon

Best for: Mono or fluoro, light to medium applications. Simple and reliable. Not ideal for braid.

Strength: ⭐⭐⭐ (95% of line strength when tied correctly)

1. Thread 6 inches of line through the hook eye.
2. Wrap the tag end around the main line 5-6 times (more for light line, fewer for heavy).
3. Pass tag end through the loop nearest the hook eye.
4. Pass tag end back through the large loop just created (this is the "improved" part).
5. Wet the knot with saliva, pull slowly until tight. Trim tag end close.
2. Palomar Knot — Best for braided line to lure

Best for: Braid or fluoro to hook or lure. Easiest strongest knot. The go-to for braid.

Strength: ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐ (virtually 100% of line strength)

1. Double 6 inches of line and pass the loop through the hook eye.
2. Tie a simple overhand knot with the doubled line (but don't tighten — leave a loop).
3. Pass the hook through the loop.
4. Wet and pull both tag end and main line simultaneously. Hook should center below the knot.
5. Trim tag end.
💡 Note
The Palomar requires passing the hook through a loop — doesn't work well for lures with large body or treble hooks where the loop won't pass over easily. Use Uni knot in that case.
3. Uni Knot — Versatile all-purpose

Best for: All line types to hooks, lures, or swivels. Works on mono, fluoro, and braid. Very reliable.

Strength: ⭐⭐⭐⭐ (90-95% line strength)

1. Thread 6+ inches of line through hook eye. Double back parallel to main line, creating a loop.
2. Wrap the tag end around both lines and through the loop 4-6 times.
3. Wet the knot and pull the tag end to tighten the coils together.
4. Slide the knot down to the hook eye by pulling the main line.
5. Pull firmly to seat. Trim tag end close.
4. Blood Knot — Joining two similar-diameter lines

Best for: Joining two lengths of monofilament or fluorocarbon of similar diameter. Building custom leaders. Adding tippet to fly leader.

Strength: ⭐⭐⭐⭐ (85-90% line strength)

1. Overlap the two line ends by about 6 inches.
2. Wrap one tag end around the other line 4-5 times, bring back through the center opening.
3. Wrap the other tag end around the first line 4-5 times, bring back through the SAME center opening but from the opposite side.
4. The two tag ends should exit the center in opposite directions.
5. Wet thoroughly, pull both main lines simultaneously. Trim both tag ends.
5. Double Uni Knot — Connecting braid to fluorocarbon leader

Best for: Connecting braid mainline to fluoro leader. The most important line-to-line knot for modern anglers. Also works for mono-to-mono.

Strength: ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐ (Strong enough for any application)

1. Overlap the two lines by 12 inches, holding in the middle.
2. Tie a Uni knot with the braid around the fluoro: fold braid back on itself, wrap 4 times through the loop, tighten down (but not to fluoro yet).
3. Tie a Uni knot with the fluoro around the braid: same process, 4 wraps. Tighten separately.
4. Now pull both main lines in opposite directions — the two uni knots slide together and lock against each other.
5. Trim both tag ends close. The finished knot passes smoothly through rod guides.
💡 Practice Tip
Watch YouTube videos for each knot — seeing hands tie them is much faster than reading. Then practice until you can tie them smoothly in 30 seconds each. Your fish-landing rate will improve immediately.

Salmon and Steelhead
  • Combined Angling Tag: Required to target or retain salmon, steelhead, sturgeon. Buy with your annual license.
  • Wild vs. Hatchery: In most Oregon waters, only hatchery salmon and steelhead (with clipped adipose fin) may be retained. Wild fish (intact adipose fin) must be released unharmed immediately. The adipose fin is the small fin behind the dorsal fin.
  • Bag limits: Typically 2 adult salmon or steelhead per day, combined. Check species-specific and water-specific rules.
  • Jack salmon: Small salmon (under 24 inches) that return after 1 year at sea. May have different retention rules — check regs.
Sturgeon (Columbia River)
  • Slot limit: Only sturgeon 44-50 inches fork length (tip of lower jaw to fork of tail) may be retained. All other sizes must be released carefully.
  • Fork length: Measured from tip of lower jaw to fork in tail fin (not the whiskers/rostrum). Have a measuring tape ready — oversized fish are as common as legal fish.
  • Season: Check current ODFW season — retention seasons can be as short as a few days. Confirm before going.
  • Releasing oversized sturgeon: Keep in water as much as possible. Do not lift by the gills or tail. Use a large net or cradle. Revive by moving back and forth in water before releasing.
Key Clatsop County Regulation Zones
  • Columbia River: Complex multi-jurisdiction rules (Oregon, Washington, federal tribal rights). Check the Columbia River Supplement section of ODFW regs carefully. Rules differ by species, season, and specific river section.
  • Ocean regulations: Oregon ocean salmon regulations are set jointly by the Pacific Fishery Management Council (PFMC) and may change year to year based on stock assessments. Check the Marine Zone section.
  • Coastal rivers: Each coastal river (Necanicum, Youngs, Klaskanine) may have specific rules. Always check the Coast Streams section for the specific river you plan to fish.
Catch-and-Release Best Practices
  • Use barbless hooks when required (required on many C&R-only waters) or pinch barbs for ey�sy removal
  • Keep fish in water as much as possible — a fish out of water is a fish suffocating
  • Wet your hands before touching fish — dry hands remove the protective slime coat
  • Use forceps/pliers to remove hooks quickly — minimize handling time
  • Don't squeeze the fish — support horizontally, never hang vertically by jaw
  • For deep-hooked fish: cut the line rather than digging for the hook. Circle hooks help prevent deep hooking.
  • Revive fish before releasing — hold in current until it swims away strongly

Ocean/Charter Fishing: What to Bring
  • The charter provides: Rod, reel, bait, tackle, and usually a cooler for your fish
  • You bring: Oregon angling license + Combined Angling Tag, motion sickness meds (take the night before — too late if taken day-of), layers (it's cold on the ocean), sunscreen, food and drinks, dry bag for valuables
  • Seasickness tip: Dramamine (original) taken the evening before works far better than taking it morning-of. Scopolamine patch (prescription) is the most effective option for chronic sufferers.

Waders Buying Guide
  • Neoprene waders: Warm (good for winter steelhead in cold water), buoyant, durable. Heavier. 3mm for Clatsop County winter use.
  • Breathable waders: Lighter, comfortable in warmer weather, packable. Less warm but comfortable spring through fall. Gore-Tex = most breathable but expensive. Cheaper membranes work fine for occasional use.
  • Boot-foot: Boots attached — convenient, warm, no separate boot needed. Heavier.
  • Stockingfoot: Neoprene sock, separate wading boots required. Better fit, more ankle support, lighter. Recommended for technical wading.
Other Essential Gear
  • Landing net: Rubber mesh nets are mandatory for catch-and-release (preserve fish slime). Frabill or Ego nets with rubber mesh.
  • Fish grip: Plastic lip gripper (Bass Pro or Rapala) — hold fish safely without removing slime. Must for bass and large fish.
  • Forceps/pliers: 7-inch stainless forceps ($8) are the single most-used item in your tackle box. Use for hook removal, crimping split shot, cutting line.
  • Tackle bag vs. box: Soft tackle bag for wading (straps over shoulder). Hard box for boat or bank fishing. Plano boxes with adjustable dividers for organization.
