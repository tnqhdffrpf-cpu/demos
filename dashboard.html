★ Big Story of the Week

UAE exits OPEC after nearly 60 years — covered by Washington Post, CNBC, NPR, NBC News, Al Jazeera, Bloomberg, Axios, Reuters, and CFR.

Why it matters: The UAE has the second-largest spare capacity inside the cartel after Saudi Arabia — the very lever OPEC uses to set marginal prices. Pulling that lever out of the room weakens Riyadh's pricing power right as a war on Iran is roiling the Strait of Hormuz. This isn't a one-news-cycle story; it rewrites how oil markets clear for years.
Prediction: Grows. Expect rolling coverage through the May 1 effective date, then a second wave when OPEC+ holds its first post-UAE meeting and Saudi Arabia signals whether it tries to discipline the market or accommodate the new reality. Volatility in Brent is the tell.
What changed and why now

The UAE has chafed at production caps for years and pushed for higher quotas; Saudi Arabia pushed back. Add political friction (opposing sides in Yemen, escalating economic competition) and the Iran/Hormuz crisis, and the UAE's exit is a clean break rather than a surprise — but the timing is what gives it weight.

Market read: pricing power, spare capacity, volatility

Removing the UAE's spare-capacity buffer means OPEC has less ammunition to absorb supply shocks. Long term, more barrels on the market is bearish; short term, the loss of coordination is bullish for volatility. Either way, hedgers and airlines are recalibrating.

Bloomberg →
The geopolitical angle

Al Jazeera and CFR both frame the exit as a structural shift — the cartel's leader losing its most important partner during a regional war. CFR notes this could accelerate a multi-polar oil market where producer alignment is more transactional and less institutional.
